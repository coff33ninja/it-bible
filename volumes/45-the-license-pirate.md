## The License Pirate

---

**BEFORE YOU DOWNLOAD 'FREE' CORPORATE SOFTWARE FROM A TORRENT SITE...**

- You downloaded "Adobe Photoshop for free" from a site with 47 pop-up ads and a URL that ends in `.ru`. It is not Photoshop. It is malware disguised as Photoshop. The "crack.exe" you ran is now mining cryptocurrency on your work laptop. The electricity bill increase will tip off the IT department before the antivirus does.
- "I just needed it for one project" — that one project now requires a full security audit of your machine, a reimage, and a report to management. The cost of the audit is 40 times the cost of a legitimate license. Your "free" software is the most expensive software the company has ever purchased.
- You installed software from a USB stick that a colleague "got from a friend." The software is unlicensed, untracked, and unpatched. It contains a known vulnerability from 2019 that is being actively exploited. You have introduced a zero-day (in terms of your company's awareness) into the network.
- "It is abandonware, nobody sells it anymore" — abandonware is still copyrighted. The copyright did not expire because the company went out of business. You are still infringing. The intellectual property still belongs to someone. Abandonment does not mean public domain.

*Note: "Free" software from unofficial sources is not a discount. It is a liability with a download button. The license cost is the price of admission. The cost of a breach is the penalty for skipping it.*

---

**BEFORE YOU USE YOUR PERSONAL LICENSE FOR COMMERCIAL WORK...**

- You bought a personal license for $10. The commercial license costs $500. You are using the personal license at work. The licensing agreement explicitly prohibits commercial use. You clicked "I agree" without reading. You agreed to the restriction.
- A software audit found your unlicensed installation. The vendor is demanding payment for the commercial license plus a penalty for unlicensed use. The total is $2,000. The commercial license was $500. Your "saving" of $490 has cost the company $1,500 in penalties.
- You installed software on 15 machines using a single personal license. The license agreement allows 1 installation. You are using 15. The vendor audit counts. The penalty is calculated per installation per year of unlicensed use. The invoice is now a six-figure number.
- "Everybody does it" — everybody does not do it. Everybody who got caught said "everybody does it." The audit found you. The fine is not based on how common the practice is. It is based on how many unlicensed installations you had. "Everybody" is not a legal defence.

*Note: A personal license is personal. Work is not personal. If you use it for work, you need a work license. The distinction is not a technicality. It is the entire basis of software licensing.*

---

**BEFORE YOU SHARE YOUR LICENSE KEY ON THE COMPANY CHAT...**

- You posted the license key in the general channel. The key is now in the chat history, searchable by every employee past and present. The vendor's license management system detected the key being used from 12 different IP addresses. The key has been revoked. Everyone who was using it is now unlicensed.
- "It is just a volume license key, it is meant to be shared" — volume license keys are meant to be managed, not shared. They have activation limits. They have audit trails. They have consequences when abused. The key allowed 50 activations. You shared it with 200 people. The key is now blocked.
- You shared a screenshot that included the license key in the background. The key was visible for 3 seconds in a meeting recording. The recording was transcribed. The transcription was indexed. The key was extracted by automated scanning. Your software asset management is now an incident response.
- "I will just generate a new key" — you cannot. The key is tied to the company's volume licensing agreement. The agreement has a finite number of activations. Each time you share a key and it gets revoked, you consume one of those activations. You are burning through the company's license allocation.

*Note: A license key is not a conversation starter. It is a credential. Treat it with the same care as a password. Because in terms of audit liability, it is more expensive than most passwords.*

---

**BEFORE YOU INSTALL SOFTWARE WITHOUT IT APPROVAL...**

- "I installed it because I needed it" — you installed an application that conflicts with the company's security software. The security software now crashes on startup. The endpoint protection is disabled. Your machine is now the weakest link in the network. All because you needed a font manager.
- You installed a browser extension that "helps with productivity." The extension reads all the pages you visit. It sends your browsing data to a third-party server. The data includes your company's internal applications, customer data, and credentials. The extension is the productivity tool of the person who bought your data.
- You downloaded a "system cleaner" that promised to speed up your computer. It deleted system files that the antivirus depended on. The antivirus can no longer run. The system cleaner was not cleaning. It was dismantling your defences.
- You installed remote desktop software to access your work computer from home. The software you chose does not use encryption. Your remote session is being broadcast in plain text over the internet. Every keystroke, every password, every document you access is visible to anyone monitoring network traffic.

*Note: IT approval exists because IT has been cleaning up the mess from unapproved installations for 30 years. The approval process is not a gate. It is a safety inspection. Skip it at your own risk.*

---

**BEFORE YOU USE OPEN SOURCE SOFTWARE WITHOUT CHECKING THE LICENSE...**

- "It is open source, so it is free to use" — open source means the source code is open. It does not mean free for commercial use. The GPL requires you to release your source code if you distribute the software. Your company's proprietary codebase just became open source because you did not read the license.
- You used an MIT-licensed library. You complied with the license terms. One of its dependencies is AGPL. The AGPL license requires you to provide source code to any user who accesses the software over a network. Your internal tool is now a legal liability because you did not check the dependency tree.
- You copied code from a blog post. The blog post did not include a license. Copyright law defaults to "all rights reserved." You do not have permission to use that code. The blog author can issue a DMCA takedown. Your production deployment could be shut down because you copied a code snippet without reading the terms.
- "It is on GitHub, so it is public domain" — GitHub is a hosting platform, not a license grant. The absence of a license file means the default copyright restrictions apply. You cannot use, modify, or distribute code that does not have an explicit license. The public repository is not a public domain dedication.

*Note: Open source is not free software. It is licensed software with specific terms. The license defines what you can do. If you have not read the license, you have not legally acquired the software.*

---

**BEFORE YOU EXTEND A TRIAL PERIOD INDEFINITELY...**

- You found a "trial reset" script that extends the 30-day trial indefinitely. The trial reset works by deleting registry keys. It also deleted your antivirus configuration. Your trial software is working. Your protection is not. The trade-off was not worth it.
- You have been using a "trial" for 18 months. The trial expired 17 months ago. You are running software that stopped receiving updates when the trial ended. The software has 17 months of unpatched vulnerabilities. The trial never ends. The vulnerability window never closes.
- "I will buy it when I am sure it works" — you have been sure for 6 months. You are not going to buy it. You are going to keep using the cracked trial until the vendor detects the tampering and sends a legal notice. The "trial" ends when the lawsuit arrives.
- You edited the hosts file to block the software from phoning home. The software phones home to check for license validation. It also phones home to check for security updates. You blocked both. The trial keeps working. So does the zero-day exploit that was patched 3 months ago.

*Note: A trial is an evaluation period, not a permanent state. If you need the software for more than 30 days, buy it. Or accept that you are running unlicensed, unpatched, and unprotected software. The trial ends. The risk does not.**
